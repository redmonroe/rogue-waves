# Rogue wave prediction rewards auditors, not inventors

Rogue wave probability prediction is a real, open and uncrowded problem, but it is the wrong place to look for maritime impact, and only a narrow version of it is tractable on a 4-core, 15 GB machine. The best published model, the Häfner et al. 2023 formula, has never been tested outside the single buoy network it was trained on, and that gap is open to a careful outsider with the public code and data. The case against going further is strong. The model moves per-wave probability by about a factor of ten around a base rate near one wave in ten thousand, no forecast centre, routing firm or class society has adopted it in three years, and accident investigators consistently prescribe stronger windows, higher air gaps and better passage planning, never better wave forecasts. Catalogued rogue-wave harm runs at roughly 40 deaths and 3 sinkings a year worldwide, about half of it at the shoreline, against 43 to 90 total ship losses a year from all causes. The verdict: worth doing as a time-boxed, reproducible audit of the published formula; not worth doing as a search for a better predictor. If the goal is a maritime problem whose solution someone will use, container stowage benchmarks and reproducible weather-routing assessment are better targets. One caution governs everything below: the research notes behind this report rest almost entirely on web-search summaries and snippets, because full-text fetches failed for every publisher host tried. No paper was read in full. Only the measurements on the local files and the local Mathlib install are first-hand.

## One formula still leads a thin, uncrowded field

### Three years of follow-ups never left the CDIP buoy network

Häfner, Gemmrich and Jochum published their symbolic model in PNAS on 20 November 2023. It predicts the probability that a wave exceeds twice the significant wave height (crest-to-trough, H/Hs > 2.0) from four sea-state drivers: crest-trough correlation r, steepness, relative depth and directional spread ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2306275120)). It builds on the group's 2021 finding that r is "the dominating parameter in all studied conditions, water depths, and locations, explaining about a factor of 10 in rogue wave risk variation" ([Scientific Reports 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-89359-1)). The validation inside the paper is internal: the model is trained on some subsets of the CDIP buoy data and scored on held-out subsets and stations of the same network.

Two direct follow-ups exist, and **neither is an independent validation**. The original group drove the formula with ECMWF hindcast spectra instead of buoy-derived sea states and reported that it outscored every other model tested, that ECMWF's own freak-wave diagnostic "does not predict the occurrence of rogue waves", and that the empirical model "should be considered for operational implementation" ([Cicon et al., JPO 2024](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/phoc/54/10/JPO-D-24-0013.1.xml)). An overlapping team had already shown that a regional WAVEWATCH III model forecasts r only "with moderate accuracy", far below its skill for significant wave height ([Cicon et al., Weather and Forecasting 2023](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/wefo/38/11/WAF-D-23-0074.1.xml)). The single outside-group paper, from the University of South Florida, reused the same FOWD-CDIP records at four stations and concluded that "the crest-trough correlation parameter alone is not a good rogue wave indicator, but its temporal variability is" ([Azevedo et al., Ocean Science 2026](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/22/367/2026/)). Its referees pushed back hard: one said the authors had misread Häfner et al., who never claimed a threshold at r > 0.6, and that the hindcast's expected maximum envelope height cannot be compared directly with buoy observations ([peer review file](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/22/367/2026/os-22-367-2026-discussion.html)). That paper tests r as an indicator at four stations; it does not test the full formula. The notes found no application of the formula to North Sea platforms, ship radar, stereo video or satellite data. That absence comes from about thirty topical searches, not from a citation sweep, so a missed validation paper is possible.

### Newer models answer a different question

The most publicised post-2023 result is a neural network that reads a buoy's time series and warns of a rogue wave minutes ahead. It catches about three in four rogue waves one minute out and about 73% five minutes out, trained on 14 million half-hour samples from 172 CDIP buoys ([Breunung and Balachandran, Scientific Reports 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-66315-3)). That is deterministic short-horizon warning, a separate problem from sea-state-level probability, and press coverage routinely conflates the two. Its authors note that a classifier that always answers "no rogue wave" scores 99.86% accuracy; the false-alarm rate at realistic base rates was not retrievable, and it is the number that decides whether the system is usable.

On the physics side, the weight of recent field evidence favours linear superposition shaped by bandwidth plus second-order effects. An 18-year analysis of about 27,500 laser records from the Ekofisk platform found no evidence of modulational instability ([Georgia Tech release on Knobler et al. 2025](https://research.gatech.edu/rogue-waves-arent-anomolies-theyre-just-bad-day-sea)). The main counter-evidence is a Southern Ocean stereo-camera campaign that measured kurtosis of 3.4 and elevated rogue probability in young wind-driven seas ([Toffoli et al., PRL 2024](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.132.154101)), and a 2026 review reports that measured occurrence often exceeds linear Gaussian theory in storms and crossing seas ([Ocean Engineering 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0029801826007225)). Both camps can be right in their own regimes: buoy and platform archives are dominated by coastal and North Sea conditions and under-sample strongly forced young seas. Agentic equation discovery reached water-wave theory in October 2026, when a multi-agent system produced a new verified scattering amplitude ([Zhou et al., arXiv 2610.00435](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.00435)), but the notes found no LLM-driven or agentic result on rogue-wave probability data.

### Not solved, and not crowded where it counts

The problem is unsolved in any practical sense. The best model explains about one order of magnitude of variation, lacks validation outside one buoy network, and has no operational deployment the notes could find. It is also uncrowded: roughly half a dozen identifiable groups have published on data-driven sea-state probability since 2023 (Victoria/Copenhagen, Maryland, Georgia Tech with Technion, Melbourne, South Florida, and a coastal freak-wave group), most reuse the same CDIP archive, and no shared benchmark or scoring rule exists. That count under-represents Europe and Asia and is not defensible as a census. The broader rogue-wave field in optics and integrable systems is large and crowded, but it is a different field. The emptiness of this niche is ambiguous evidence. It signals opportunity, and it also signals that specialists see limited return.

## Forecast centres publish freak-wave fields that no one demonstrably acts on

### ECMWF runs the only named product, and independent tests give it no skill

ECMWF is the only centre the notes could document as running an operational "freak wave warning system". It derives the Benjamin-Feir Index, a simplified kurtosis and an expected maximum individual wave height from the predicted spectrum; ECMWF's documentation says the full kurtosis expression "is far too involved" for operations ([IFS documentation CY43R1](https://www.ecmwf.int/sites/default/files/elibrary/2016/79992-ifs-documentation-cy43r1-part-vii-ecmwf-wave-model_1.pdf)). The headline output is an expectation, written as the significant wave height times a modest factor ([Bidlot, ECMWF wave parameters](https://confluence.ecmwf.int/download/attachments/59774192/wave_parameters.pdf)), so it largely re-expresses the significant wave height forecast. Commercial data resellers redistribute it as a generic "maximum wave height" field ([Meteomatics](https://www.meteomatics.com/en/api/available-parameters/marine-parameters/wave-height/)). The independent evaluations cited above say this family of diagnostics does not discriminate rogue-prone sea states, and the 2023 Weather and Forecasting paper states flatly that "as of now, there is no robust rogue wave probability forecast". The notes found no operational rogue-wave probability product at NOAA/NCEP, the Met Office, MET Norway, DWD, JMA or Environment Canada, only research prototypes such as a 2019 Naval Research Laboratory utility ([NRL report](https://www7320.nrlssc.navy.mil/pubs/2019/orzech4-2019.pdf)). This is absence of evidence from a limited search.

### Routing runs on wave-height thresholds and ship-motion hazards

No primary documentation shows any routing provider exposing a rogue-wave index as a routing constraint. StormGeo's onboard software flags roll resonance, surf-riding and wave impact against wave-height thresholds ([BVS 8.5 user guide](https://www.datocms-assets.com/119923/1713514323-stormgeo-bvs-8-5-user-guide.pdf)); Sofar's Wayfinder restricts paths "to regions where forecasted wave heights and wind speed remain below captain-specified thresholds" ([Sofar](https://www.sofarocean.com/posts/dynamic-route-optimization-finding-the-shortest-path-with-wayfinder?hss_channel=tw-375814528)). The one claim that StormGeo and DTN "incorporate extreme wave probability products" comes from an unsourced February 2026 web article that could not be opened ([New Space Economy](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/02/26/rogue-waves-and-satellites-how-space-technology-is-changing-what-we-know-about-the-oceans-most-dangerous-phenomenon/)); treat it as unconfirmed. Routing demonstrably works on the background sea state: the revised industry wave scatter diagram, built from AIS tracks of more than 21,000 cargo ships, assigns lower probabilities to the highest sea states than its predecessor, partly because ships avoid them ([ClassNK on IACS Rec. 34 Rev.2](https://www.classnk.or.jp/hp/pdf/research/rd/2023/07_e08.pdf)).

### Design codes absorb rogue waves into long-term extremes

Offshore and ship design does not use sea-state-conditional rogue probabilities. Norwegian and ISO practice checks structures against 100-year waves and, as an accidental limit state, 10,000-year waves, with a positive air gap required for the 10,000-year crest ([DNV report for BSEE](https://www.bsee.gov/sites/bsee.gov/files/research-reports//677aa.pdf)). DNV-RP-C205 carries a freak-wave subsection that says "neither the probability of occurrence of these waves nor their physics is well understood" ([DNV-RP-C205](https://rules.dnv.com/docs/pdf/DNVPM/codes/docs/2010-10/rp-c205.pdf)). The standards community's current position is that rogue waves are "largely accounted for in established design practices" ([ISSC 2025 Committee I.1](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-95-2668-0_1)). When waves have killed people, regulators responded structurally. After the COSLInnovator fatality in 2015, the Norwegian authority found the rules "too vague" on horizontal wave loads, and DNV GL issued new air-gap and wave-impact guidance ([Havtil investigation](https://www.havtil.no/en/supervision/investigation-reports/2016/cosl-drilling---coslinnovator---investigation-of-incident-with-fatal-consequences/)). After the Viking Polaris death in 2022, the Norwegian investigators recommended stronger window standards and stated that the crew "did not have the sufficient premises for predicting the risk" ([NSIA report 2023/06](https://nsia.no/Marine/Published-reports/2023-06)). A design that tolerates the wave makes the forecast unnecessary.

Offshore operations follow the same logic. Lifts, transfers and tows are planned against significant-wave-height limits multiplied by forecast-uncertainty factors ([summary of DNV-ST-N001](https://nodynamics.com/knowledge-hub/dnv-st-n001-marine-operations/)), and the wave-by-wave technology that operators buy is deterministic radar-based motion prediction, selected in August 2026 for a Dudgeon wind-farm service vessel ([Offshore Magazine](https://www.offshore-mag.com/renewable-energy/news/55401880/esvagt-selects-motion-prediction-radar-for-dudgeon-wind-farm-support-vessel)). A rogue-probability forecast has no slot in that framework.

### A better formula changes no decision until someone builds the decision

The arithmetic explains the indifference. Under Rayleigh statistics the per-wave probability of H > 2 Hs is exp(-8), about 3.3 in 10,000, and the Häfner model treats that as a ceiling reached only in its most extreme conditions ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2306275120)). At a 10-second mean period a fixed point sees about 8,600 waves a day, so a factor-of-ten modulation moves the expected count from roughly one such wave every four days to roughly three a day (this report's arithmetic on the cited figures). A wave of twice the significant height in a 1.5 m sea is 3 m and harms no ship. To be actionable the probability must be combined with significant wave height into an absolute-height exceedance over a route segment, which collapses most of its information back into the forecast users already act on. The binding constraints are the accuracy of model-predicted spectral shape and the absence of a decision that would change, not the formula mapping parameters to probability. The realistic adoption route is a diagnostic field added to ECMWF or NCEP output by a science-led change, which vendors would inherit for free, exactly as they inherit the existing maximum-wave-height field. The notes found no cost-loss study of rogue-wave forecasts for any user, no published acceptance criteria, and no statement from ECMWF responding to the 2024 recommendation.

## Catalogued rogue waves kill about 40 people a year

Media-based catalogues give the only global totals. For 2005 to 2021 they record **429 damaging events, 658 deaths, 575 injuries, 102 ships damaged and 55 sunk**, across small fishing boats, large ships and the shoreline ([Didenkulova et al., NHESS 2023](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/23/1653/2023/)). That is about 39 deaths and 3 sinkings a year. About 52% of those events were at the coast ([NHESS 2023 preprint](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2022-215/nhess-2022-215.pdf)), where the victims are rock fishers and beach walkers with no ship, no routing and no use for an open-ocean forecast. The catalogues themselves warn that "the majority of events is based on eyewitness accounts, and as a rule is not confirmed by direct measurements" ([catalogue abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020EGUGA..22.4457D/abstract)), so the totals overstate through unverified labelling and understate through unreported events.

Attribution by investigators is far weaker than attribution by the press. The well-documented big-ship cases since 1995 end in broken windows and zero to two deaths: Louis Majesty in 2010 (2 killed), COSLInnovator in 2015 (1 killed), Viking Polaris in 2022 (1 killed). Investigation bodies almost never write "rogue wave". The NTSB attributed Norwegian Dawn's 2005 damage to waves breaking over the bow in severe weather ([NTSB MAB-05/03](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/MAB0503.pdf)). The peer-reviewed reconstruction of Louis Majesty states that "due to the lack of measurements, it is impossible to establish the nature of the wave" ([Cavaleri et al. 2012](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2012JC007923)). For El Faro, the NTSB concluded a rogue wave was not a factor ([NTSB MAR-17/01](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/accidentreports/reports/mar1701.pdf)). The famous claim that rogue waves sank "more than 200 supertankers and container ships" is an ESA press statement about severe-weather losses in which rogue waves are "believed to be the major cause in many" ([ESA 2004](https://www.esa.int/Applications/Observing_the_Earth/Ship-sinking_monster_waves_revealed_by_ESA_satellites)); no loss list was found, and a MaxWave-linked paper gives about 22 supercarriers for 1969 to 1994 ([Rosenthal and Lehner](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225019592_Rogue_Waves_Results_of_the_MaxWave_Project)). The largest recent candidate, the MV Barima ferry capsize off Guyana in July 2026 with at least 53 dead, carried about 179 people against a manifest of 133, and its rogue-wave label comes from Wikipedia with no investigation findings located ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/21/guyana-reports-ferry-disaster-death-toll-rises-to-41); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Barima)).

Set against other maritime risks, the slice is small. Total losses of ships over 100 GT numbered 43 in 2025 and 905 over 2016 to 2025; machinery failure accounted for 1,505 incidents in 2025, collision 260 and fire or explosion 218; "rogue wave" is not a category in any of these datasets ([Allianz Safety and Shipping Review 2026](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/commercial-safety-shipping-review-2026.pdf)). Total losses fell from 105 in 2015 to 27 in 2024 under the insurer's earlier counting method, with no rogue-wave forecasting in use ([Allianz Safety and Shipping Review 2025](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/commercial-safety-shipping-review-2025.pdf)). The fishing sector alone loses more than 100,000 lives a year on one estimate ([Pew](https://www.pew.org/en/about/news-room/press-releases-and-statements/2022/11/03/more-than-100000-people-die-annually-across-global-fishing-sector-new-research-shows)). Container losses, 1,478 boxes in 2025 with one capsize supplying 43% of them ([SWZ Maritime](https://swzmaritime.nl/news/2026/07/01/1478-containers-lost-at-sea-in-2025-above-three-year-average/)), trace to parametric rolling in regular wave trains, sometimes in mild seas, and not to rogue waves ([TT Club on the TopTier project](https://www.ttclub.com/news-and-resources/news/article/tt-talk-industry-reports-shed-new-light-on-container-losses-at-sea/)). Shore deaths on single coasts rival the global ship figure: Australian rock fishing averages about 13 deaths a year ([Surf Life Saving Australia](https://www.surflifesaving.com.au/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2020/05/Surf-Life-Saving-Australia-Rock-Fishing-Coastal-Safety-Brief-2020.pdf)).

The accident-database evidence points toward a different product. An analysis of weather-related casualties from Lloyd's records found that they cluster in steep, crossing and rapidly changing seas, many at significant wave heights below 3 m ([Toffoli et al. 2005](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/230636.pdf); [citing preprint](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2019-399/nhess-2019-399.pdf)). That is a dangerous-sea-state hazard computable from standard spectral forecasts. The one long buoy study of trends found rogue-wave occurrence "mostly decreasing" while severity increased ([Cattrell et al. 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6418161/)); the climate signal sits in rising background wave heights, which are forecastable, and not in the rogue ratio.

## Five audits fit the machine; the decisive test does not

### What the downloaded files show

The local dataset matches its description, and the checks below were re-run for this report. The 157 parquet files hold **14,985,244 rows and 106 columns**; 119,500 rows (0.80%) contain a wave with H/Hs above 2.0; and 21,848 rows (0.146%) exceed a crest-height ratio of 1.25 under a second label the files carry but the published model never uses (measured locally; [code and data](https://github.com/dionhaefner/rogue-wave-discovery)). The notes add two structural facts from the same files. A Southern California box holds 52% of all rows and the US West Coast 71%, while 40 East and Gulf coast stations contribute under 5%. So "157 stations" overstates diversity: the effective sample is the California and Hawaii swell climate. The files end in May 2022.

### The audits that need no new data

Five contributions follow from those facts, and none needs more than the public files. The first is calibration and reliability of the closed-form model by station, region and depth, scored with a proper scoring rule in place of aggregate likelihoods. The second is a leave-one-region-out test that removes the Southern California block. The third is a crest-height version of the analysis, which matters because design and damage depend on crest elevation and the authors themselves doubt "whether the common rogue wave definition as any wave exceeding a certain height threshold is meaningful in practice" ([Scientific Reports 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-89359-1)). The fourth is sensitivity to the hard-coded quality filters (the paper's code drops sea states with excess kurtosis beyond ±1 and skewness beyond ±0.5, confirmed in the local copy of `constants.py`) and to the independence assumption in the 100-wave aggregation, which the authors say underestimates per-wave probability in grouped seas ([PMC copy of the PNAS paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10691345/)). The fifth is testing the drop-then-rise pattern in r that Azevedo et al. reported at four stations across all 157; a referee asked for exactly this to be explored in more detail ([author response](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-2031/egusphere-2025-2031-AR2.pdf)). A sixth, an out-of-time test on the roughly 4.5 years of CDIP displacement data recorded since the files end, is the most valuable and the least certain: it requires running the open FOWD pipeline ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/4628203)) on raw CDIP files, and its cost on four cores is unknown.

Compute is not the obstacle for any of these. Reading two columns from all 157 files took 0.35 seconds on the 4-core machine in this report's check. The paper's full pipeline is another matter: its README says all 24 experiments take "about 2 days on a 16-core machine" and need at least 64 GB of RAM, and the symbolic-regression notebook is configured for 48 processes ([repository](https://github.com/dionhaefner/rogue-wave-discovery)). The notes estimate that a column-pruned loader fits in 15 GB and that a single re-fit takes about 8 hours on four cores; neither estimate was tested. Scoring and auditing the formula is comfortable. A wide model search is out of reach, and a local desktop with more memory changes that only at the margin.

### The test that would settle the question needs data no one can download

The decisive experiment is validation on a different sensor type, and it is blocked. Radar stations in the southern North Sea recorded more rogue waves than co-located buoys ([Teutsch et al. 2020](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/20/2665/2020/)), a later communication attributes the gap to measurement technique ([NHESS 2024](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/24/2065/2024/nhess-24-2065-2024.pdf)), and the PNAS authors themselves list buoy sensor bias as a threat to generalisation. The model's "Rayleigh is an upper bound" result could therefore partly reflect buoy under-measurement of sharp crests. Fixed-platform laser and radar records sit with Shell, the Ekofisk operator and similar holders. NDBC does not transmit raw time series ([NDBC technical document](https://www.ndbc.noaa.gov/wavemeas.pdf)), and the open Sofar archive holds hourly bulk parameters ([AWS registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/sofar-spotter-archive/)), which cannot label individual waves. Australia's IMOS network archives raw 2.5 Hz displacements under CC BY ([IMOS QA/QC manual](https://content.aodn.org.au/Documents/IMOS/Facilities/coastal_wave_buoys/IMOS_Coastal_Wave_Buoys_QAQC_Documentation_v1.0.pdf)) and is the best lead for a non-US test, but whether those raw files are publicly downloadable is unverified, and they are still buoys.

### Lean fits the library gap, not the science gap

The project's pinned Mathlib has no Rayleigh, Weibull or Gumbel distribution; its distributions folder holds Bernoulli, beta, binomial, Cauchy, exponential, gamma, Gaussian, geometric, Pareto, Poisson and uniform (checked locally for this report; the notes' web-derived claim that Pareto is missing is out of date for this install). An Aalto course project to formalise the Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko theorem "is still work in progress" ([ExtremeValueProject blueprint](https://kkytola.github.io/ExtremeValueProject/blueprint)), and the notes found no formalised ocean-wave statistics in any proof assistant. Feasible targets exist: define the Rayleigh distribution and prove that the exceedance at twice the significant height equals exp(-8), prove the block-maximum identity behind the 100-wave aggregation, and prove the divergence of the published formula's spread term as spread goes to zero, which the authors acknowledge ([arXiv 2311.12579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.12579)). These are honest library contributions with a maritime theme. They say nothing about whether the model is right about the ocean, because the bottleneck is empirical: sensor bias and data coverage. Practitioners make the same point about physics formalisation generally: proof assistants "don't say anything about the physics context of results" ([arXiv 2607.06379](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.06379)). Lean belongs in this project as a side track.

## The strongest objections come from inside the field

The first objection is that the signal is near its ceiling. The largest fixed-sensor study, 122 million waves from 33 platform sites, concluded that rogue waves "are not governed by sea state parameters" and are rare members of the normal population ([Christou and Ewans 2014](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/381785.pdf)). A separate buoy study found that in bulk "only weak relationships can be seen" ([Cattrell et al. 2018](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/520662/)). The DNV-led ExWaMar project concluded that "sampling variability makes it very challenging to identify reliable warning criteria for rogue waves, particularly when temporal point data are employed" ([ISSC Committee I.1 report](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/507633/file/507633_Fulltext.pdf)). The buoy literature says sea state matters through r; the fixed-sensor literature says it barely matters at all. An outsider cannot resolve that disagreement without the industry data.

The second objection is that the remaining predictable variance plausibly sits below the sensor-bias floor of the only open dataset. Any improvement fitted to CDIP buoys inherits whatever the buoys get wrong, and nothing in the public data can separate a physical effect from an instrument effect.

The third is that a marginal skill gain has no identified user, for the reasons set out above, and the fourth is that the niche already belongs to specialists who built the model, hold the forecasting relationships and are still publishing. The fifth concerns the person. The one outside-group FOWD paper, written by a university research group, still drew substantive referee objections on indicator choice and comparability. Domain reviewers weigh measurement physics that a non-specialist lacks, and an AI assistant does not supply field experience with buoy records.

The counter-case deserves equal candour. The code and data are fully public, secondary analyses of FOWD do get published, authors and referees have named specific unexplored directions, and an independent audit has value whichever way it comes out. A formula that a forecast centre is being urged to adopt, and that rests on a sample dominated by one stretch of coast, merits a second pair of eyes. The honest summary is that the audit has real scientific value and low odds of changing a maritime decision.

## Verdict: run a time-boxed audit, or choose a problem that has users

**Rogue wave prediction is worth doing only in narrowed form.** The narrowed form is a reproducible audit of the Häfner et al. formula on the public data: per-region calibration, a leave-Southern-California-out test, the crest-height variant, filter sensitivity, and the r-variability test at all stations, with the out-of-time CDIP test as a stretch goal. Fix the scoring rules and the stopping point before looking at results, and limit the effort to about four to six weeks. Both outcomes are reportable: a formula that holds up outside Southern California is a useful confirmation, and one that fails is a finding the original authors' validation could not have produced. The deliverable is a public repository and a short technical note. The project's start-up cost is already paid, since the data, code and Lean toolchain are on disk. Before starting, spend an hour on the citation list of the PNAS paper to confirm no one has published this audit.

**It is not worth doing as a search for a better predictor.** A new formula fitted to the same buoys cannot be distinguished from sensor artefact, cannot be validated on the data that would settle the matter, and has no route to a user that does not run through ECMWF.

Neither the audit nor any alternative below touches cryptography or weapons. The nearest contact with defence is ordinary: navies consume and fund wave forecasting like other mariners. The U.S. Navy is piloting a commercial routing product ([Naval Meteorology and Oceanography Command](https://www.cnmoc.usff.navy.mil/Press-Room/News-Stories/Article/3967747/us-navy-pilots-sofars-wayfinder-to-enhance-safe-routing-at-sea/)), and search and rescue serves coast guards.

For a person whose priority is a maritime problem whose answer someone will use, the alternatives rank as follows. The ranking is this report's judgement from the notes, which assessed crowding qualitatively and did not test any of these on the machine.

| Rank | Problem | Why it ranks here | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Container stowage planning on public benchmarks | The only candidate with public instances and objective scores today: a 2024 survey publishes 162 benchmark instances and a research agenda ([survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.07573); [benchmark repository](https://github.com/maritimelab/StowagePlanning_MasterPlanning_Benchmark)), and a 2025 suite adds industrial-sized instances ([RCSPPSuite](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1366554525002650)). A better solution is verifiable by anyone, with no credentials or data requests, and optimisation code is work an AI assistant does well | Moderately crowded with operations researchers; weakest direct safety value; the path from benchmark gains to commercial planners was not researched |
| 2 | Reproducible assessment of ship weather routing | Real users and an explicit stated gap: the authors of the open VISIR-2 model say routing's potential "has not been reproducibly assessed, especially for short sea shipping" ([GMD 2024](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/17/4355/2024/)). Open code and open wave data. It also reuses the sea-state knowledge already acquired, and steep and crossing seas, where casualties cluster, can enter as a hazard layer | Commercially dominated and academically busy; requires ship-performance modelling judgement; compute and data volumes untested |
| 3 | Evaluating search-and-rescue drift-to-search pipelines | Highest safety value. A 2026 review argues that better drift accuracy does not necessarily produce better search outcomes and calls for calibration testing of drift uncertainty ([JMSE review](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1312/14/20/1886)); the OpenDrift tooling is open | Open ground-truth data are thin, since the leeway coefficients date from a 1999 compilation ([Allen and Plourde](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA366414.pdf)); coast guards are hard for an outsider to reach |
| 4 | Storm-surge prediction from tide gauges | Fully open data, small-compute templates and real coastal-agency users ([ERL 2020](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ab89d6)) | Crowded with well-resourced groups; a small effort adds a careful benchmark at best |
| 5 | Sneaker-wave hazard indices | The shoreline is where about half of catalogued rogue-wave harm occurs, few researchers work on it, and forecasters already issue statements ([OSU](https://news.oregonstate.edu/news/far-storms-fuel-sneaker-waves-along-pacific-northwest-coast-new-research-suggests)) | No open event catalogue or runup dataset was found; blocked on data |

The narrowed rogue-wave audit sits beside the third-ranked option on scientific interest and below all of the top three on the odds of being used. Parametric-roll prediction, AIS collision risk and fuel prediction are poor fits, respectively for lack of open data, reliance on modelled labels, and coarse public data. The one place the Lean setup matches a maritime-named problem is formalising Isbell's minimax solution to Bellman's "lost at sea" search problem, which appears unformalised ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Bellman%27s_lost-in-a-forest_problem)); it is a pleasant mathematics project with no practical maritime value.

## Conclusion

The research inverted the question it started with. The original framing treated the formula as the scarce thing and asked whether a better one could be found. The evidence says the formula is the cheap part. What is scarce is independent observations of extreme waves, accurate model predictions of spectral shape, and a decision framework that would act on a probability. A lone researcher can supply none of those three, but can supply the thing the field has skipped: a disinterested check of a model its own authors are recommending for operational use.

The broader lesson for choosing a problem is that maritime safety gains have come from structure and procedure, and accident reports repeat this on every page. Problems with a public benchmark or a named operational gap give a non-specialist something no amount of ingenuity gives in rogue-wave physics: a result that someone else can verify and has a reason to want. Run the audit for what it teaches about the data and the field, with a firm end date. Then take the skills to a problem with users.
