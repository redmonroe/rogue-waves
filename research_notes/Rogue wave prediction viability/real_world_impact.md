Verification note (applies to every section): research date 26 November 2026. ## 1. What documented ship losses, damage incidents and fatalities are attributed to rogue waves?

### Takeaway
The list of well-documented rogue-wave incidents is short, and most cases involve broken windows, flooding of a bridge or cabins, and zero to two deaths; the large death tolls sit in a handful of older or poorly documented losses (München 1978, Ocean Ranger 1982) and in one unconfirmed 2026 ferry capsize. Media-based catalogues give roughly 40 deaths and about 3 vessels sunk per year worldwide across all vessel sizes and the shoreline combined.

### Cited Findings

Named incidents (chronological)

- MS München, December 1978 (North Atlantic): the Bremerhaven Maritime Board of Inquiry stated "It was impossible to explain the cause of the sinking with a sufficient degree of certainty"; its most likely cause was heavy sea wash leading to loss of radio equipment and machinery and slow sinking. The rogue-wave explanation is the "widely accepted theory", not the board's finding. — [Wikipedia: MS München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS_M%C3%BCnchen); background at [Hapag-Lloyd](https://www.hapag-lloyd.com/en/company/about-us/newsletter/2018/11/the-last-journey-of-the--muenchen-.html)
- Ocean Ranger, February 1982 (Grand Banks, semi-submersible rig): 84 lives lost. The rig reported storm seas of 55 ft (17 m) with the odd wave up to 65 ft (20 m). Popular sources name a 26 m rogue wave as the principal cause; that figure comes from blogs and should be treated with caution. — [Wikipedia: Ocean Ranger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_Ranger); [popular account](https://www.rosslaird.com/articles/blog/rogue-waves/)
- Ekofisk 2/4-A platform, 3 January 1984: a wave crest broke a light wall on the lower deck; no fatalities reported. — [Liu, "A chronology of freaque wave encounters" (NOAA GLERL, 2007)](https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/pubs/fulltext/2007/20070019.pdf)
- Draupner platform, 1 January 1995 (North Sea): laser-measured maximum wave height 25.6 m, the first rogue wave measured in the open ocean. The platform sustained only minor damage because it had been built for larger waves. — [Wikipedia: Draupner wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draupner_wave)
- Schiehallion FPSO, 9 November 1998: a wave reached 18 m above the waterline. — [Liu chronology (NOAA GLERL)](https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/pubs/fulltext/2007/20070019.pdf)
- Bremen and Caledonian Star, early 2001 (South Atlantic): both cruise ships had bridge windows smashed by waves reported as about 30 m, roughly 1,000 km apart. Bremen drifted without navigation or propulsion for two hours. No deaths or injuries are mentioned in the sources seen. — [ESA 2004 release](https://www.esa.int/Applications/Observing_the_Earth/Ship-sinking_monster_waves_revealed_by_ESA_satellites); [Wikipedia: National Geographic Endeavour (ex Caledonian Star)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Geographic_Endeavour)
- Norwegian Dawn, 16 April 2005 (off South Carolina): windows on decks 9 and 10 damaged. Press accounts said a 70 ft (21 m) wave or a series of three. NTSB brief MAB-05/03 gave the probable cause as waves breaking over the bow during the ship's unavoidable encounter with severe weather and heavy seas, and cleared the line and captain. — [NTSB MAB-05/03](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/MAB0503.pdf); [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/2005-12-28norwegian-cruise-line-cleared-in-wave)
- Louis Majesty, 3 March 2010 (western Mediterranean): a large wave destroyed windows on deck 5; 2 passengers killed (one German, one Italian) and 14 injured. — [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2010/WORLD/europe/03/04/spain.cruise.ship.wave/?hpt=C1); [Cavaleri et al. 2012, JGR Oceans](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2012JC007923)
- COSL Innovator, 30 December 2015 (Troll field, North Sea, semi-submersible): 1 killed, 4 injured; the wave smashed 17 windows and flooded cabins. — [Norwegian PSA/Havtil investigation page](https://www.havtil.no/en/supervision/investigation-reports/2016/cosl-drilling---coslinnovator---investigation-of-incident-with-fatal-consequences/)
- Fishing vessel Progress, 2018: damage, not loss. NTSB probable cause was "an encounter with a considerably larger wave than those the vessel had been experiencing while hove to in gale-force conditions". — [NTSB MAB-19/04](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/MAB1904.pdf)
- Viking Polaris, 29 November 2022 (south-east of Cape Horn): a wave struck the port side at about 22:35 and broke seven cabin windows on deck 2. NSIA figures as relayed by trade press: 1 passenger killed, 8 injured. Initial press reports said 4 injured. — [NSIA report 2023/06](https://nsia.no/Marine/Published-reports/2023-06); [Marine Log](https://www.marinelog.com/legal/safety-and-security/video-nsia-reports-on-deadly-viking-polaris-encounter-with-a-breaking-wave/); [NBC News (initial report)](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/rogue-wave-hits-viking-cruise-ship-killing-1-passenger-injuring-4-othe-rcna59819)

2023 to 2026 incidents

- MS Maud (Hurtigruten/HX), 21 December 2023 (North Sea): a wave broke bridge windows, water caused a power failure on the bridge, and the ship was steered manually from the engine room until towed. Conditions reported as Force 8 with seas of 7 to 8 m. The owner called it a rogue wave; no serious injuries. — [gCaptain/Reuters](https://gcaptain.com/hurtigruten-cruise-ship-awaiting-tow-after-hit-from-rogue-wave/); [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/call-for-assistance-after-hurtigruten-s-maud-is-disabled-in-north-sea-storm); [MarineLink](https://www.marinelink.com/news/cruise-ship-loses-navigation-ability-510345)
- Spirit of Discovery (Saga), 4 November 2023 (Bay of Biscay): often mentioned alongside rogue-wave stories but it is a forecast-storm case. The ship lost propulsion in heavy weather; an 85-year-old passenger died and over 100 passengers (115 in some coverage) were injured. MAIB Report 6/2026 (March 2026) criticised passage planning that left the ship exposed to forecast seas of about 10 m. — [MAIB report 6/2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69aeb854bde9c3f213c899cb/2026-6-SpiritOfDiscovery-Report.pdf); [Marine Insight](https://www.marineinsight.com/cruise-passenger-dies-after-spinal-injury-during-violent-bay-of-biscay-storm-investigation-finds-care-delay/)
- Marina Beach near Southbroom, South Africa, September 2023: a suspected rogue or sneaker wave hit a beachside restaurant, injuring seven. Roi-Namur, Kwajalein Atoll, January 2024: one or more rogue or sneaker waves damaged buildings at a US Army site. Both are shore events. — [Wikipedia: List of rogue waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rogue_waves)
- Explorer of the Seas (Royal Caribbean), November 2024: reported heavy tilt from a large wave combined with strong winds, minor injuries. Sources conflict on ship name and details; unverified. — [cruisemummy.co.uk](https://www.cruisemummy.co.uk/cruise-ships-and-rogue-waves/)
- Vision of the Seas (Royal Caribbean), 20 September 2025 (Bermuda to Baltimore): press reported a "rogue wave" that heeled the ship for several minutes and threw dishes about; no injuries reported. — [Cruise Law News](https://www.cruiselawnews.com/2025/09/articles/severe-weather/rogue-wave-rocks-vision-of-the-seas/); [TravelPulse](https://www.travelpulse.com/news/cruise/rogue-wave-causes-temporary-chaos-aboard-cruise-ship)
- Puerto de la Cruz, Tenerife, 8 November 2025: a large wave swept people off a pier, killing a Dutch cruise passenger and injuring several. Shore event. — [Cruise Hive](https://www.cruisehive.com/giant-wave-crashes-onto-pier-sweeping-cruise-ship-visitors-away/191549)
- Fishing vessel lost in Icy Strait, Alaska, 1 December 2024 (five crew dead): the NTSB report (dated November 2026 per the trade summary) gives the probable cause as a loss of stability from one or several possible factors (modification, icing, flooding, shifting weights, steering casualty or unknown). No rogue-wave finding. — [SAFETY4SEA summary of NTSB report](https://safety4sea.com/ntsb-investigation-capsizing-and-sinking-of-commercial-fishing-vessel/)
- MV Barima ferry, 18 or 19 July 2026 (off the Pomeroon River, Guyana): capsized about seven miles offshore. Death toll at least 53 with roughly 50 missing as of 21 to 22 July 2026; about 179 aboard against a manifest of 133. Wikipedia says "a large wave, apparently a rogue wave" struck her. Officials also cited the incomplete manifest and possible drug use by crew; the captain and another crew member were detained after testing positive for marijuana. A separate investigation was announced; no findings located. — [Al Jazeera, 21 July 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/21/guyana-reports-ferry-disaster-death-toll-rises-to-41); [CNN, 20 July 2026](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/20/americas/guyana-shipwreck-intl); [Wikipedia: MV Barima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Barima); [St Vincent Times](https://www.stvincenttimes.com/mv-barima-tragedy-death-toll-rises-as-narcotics-manifest-failures-revealed/)

Aggregate counts from media-based catalogues

- 2005 to 2021 (Didenkulova, Didenkulova and Medvedev, NHESS 2023): 429 events that caused damage or human loss; 658 people killed, 575 injured, 102 ships damaged and 55 ships sunk ("both small fishing boats and large ships"). — [NHESS 23, 1653 (2023)](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/23/1653/2023/)
- 2011 to 2018 (Didenkulova, Ocean & Coastal Management 2020): 210 events, 386 deaths, 184 or 186 injuries (sources differ), 24 ships sunk. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338143532_Catalogue_of_rogue_waves_occurred_in_the_World_Ocean_from_2011_to_2018_reported_by_mass_media_sources); [EGU2020 presentation](https://presentations.copernicus.org/EGU2020/EGU2020-4457_presentation.pdf); [HSE summary](https://perm.hse.ru/en/news/848838402.html)
- 2006 to 2010 (Nikolkina and Didenkulova, NHESS 2011): 131 reported events, of which 78 were identified as evidence of rogue waves; the paper states 131 lives lost and 196 injured. — [NHESS 11, 2913 (2011)](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/11/2913/2011/)
- Liu (NOAA) compiled a chronology of more than 50 incidents likely associated with rogue waves. — [Liu chronology](https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/pubs/fulltext/2007/20070019.pdf); [Wikipedia: List of rogue waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rogue_waves_incidents)
- A 2025 Ocean Dynamics paper (Azevedo et al.) works from a dataset of 440 reported rogue wave incidents. — [Ocean Dynamics 75:52 (2025)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10236-025-01697-0)

### Inferences
- From the 2005 to 2021 catalogue: 658 deaths over 17 years is about 39 per year, and 55 vessels sunk is about 3 per year, worldwide, including small boats and shore victims. The 2011 to 2018 catalogue gives about 48 deaths per year. These are my arithmetic on the cited totals.
- In the well-investigated big-ship cases since 1995, the typical outcome is window or superstructure damage with 0 to 2 deaths. No modern large merchant or cruise ship loss has an investigation finding of "rogue wave" as cause in the material I found.
- If the MV Barima capsize (2026) were confirmed as wave-caused, it would by itself exceed several years of catalogued rogue-wave deaths. It also carried about 46 more people than its manifest, so overloading and crew condition are competing explanations.

### Gaps
- München crew count (commonly given as 28) and Norwegian Dawn injury count were not shown in the search extracts, so I have not stated them as verified.
- No investigation report was located for MS Maud (2023), Vision of the Seas (2025), Explorer of the Seas (2024) or MV Barima (2026).
- I found no 2023 to 2026 container ship or large cargo ship casualty attributed to a rogue wave by any source.
- The Wikipedia list's 2025 and 2026 entries did not appear in search extracts.

## 2. How reliable are the attributions? Investigation bodies versus press claims

### Takeaway
Attribution is weak. Investigation bodies almost never use the term "rogue wave"; they find "breaking wave", "heavy seas", "loss of stability" or "cause could not be determined", and they often point to design margins or passage planning instead. The academic catalogues are built from press reports and eyewitness accounts without measurements.

### Cited Findings
- NSIA on Viking Polaris: the ship "was struck by a breaking wave that, in combination with the ship's course and speed, caused the windows to shatter"; the pressure exceeded what the windows were designed for; sea conditions "were within the sea states defined in the wave scatter diagram the ship was designed for"; IACS class rules give too low a design pressure for breaking waves. NSIA holds that "a robust design that takes account of breaking waves will constitute a stronger barrier than operational measures alone." The US Coast Guard concurred. — [NSIA report page](https://nsia.no/Marine/Published-reports/2023-06); [Marine Log](https://www.marinelog.com/legal/safety-and-security/video-nsia-reports-on-deadly-viking-polaris-encounter-with-a-breaking-wave/); [USCG memo](https://www.dco.uscg.mil/Portals/9/DCO%20Documents/5p/CG-5PC/INV/docs/documents/VIKING%20POLARIS%20FAM_28DEC23_Redacted.pdf)
- Trade press on the same case: the wave "has been described as a 'rogue' wave", but the Norwegian board noted that abnormally large waves are a regular occurrence in the region. — [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/report-windows-aboard-expedition-cruise-ship-couldn-t-resist-fatal-wave)
- NTSB on Norwegian Dawn: probable cause was waves breaking over the bow in severe weather and heavy seas; no rogue-wave finding, against press reports of a 70 ft wave. — [NTSB MAB-05/03](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/MAB0503.pdf)
- München: the German board could not explain the sinking "with a sufficient degree of certainty". — [Wikipedia: MS München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS_M%C3%BCnchen)
- Louis Majesty: the owner's spokesman first said there would be no investigation; Maltese officials (flag state) later led one. The peer-reviewed reconstruction attributes the sea state to two wave systems (from SE and ENE) converging on the ship and gives a model value of about 5.13 m, but states that "due to the lack of measurements, it is impossible to establish the nature of the wave that caused the accident". — [Cavaleri et al. 2012](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2012JC007923); [Cruise Law News](https://www.cruiselawnews.com/2010/03/articles/death-injury/monster-waves-hit-louis-majesty-cruise-ship/)
- COSL Innovator: the Norwegian PSA found weaknesses in how wave-force calculations are used in mobile unit design; the rig lacked a 1.5 m air gap between the lowest deck and the highest wave crest and its superstructure was not dimensioned for horizontal wave loads. — [PSA/Havtil](https://www.havtil.no/en/supervision/investigation-reports/2016/cosl-drilling---coslinnovator---investigation-of-incident-with-fatal-consequences/); [OE Digital](https://www.oedigital.com/subsea/item/12135-psa-cosl-rig-not-built-for-big-waves)
- MAIB on Spirit of Discovery: propulsion loss in forecast heavy weather, passage planning and mass-casualty response; not a rogue-wave case. — [MAIB 6/2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69aeb854bde9c3f213c899cb/2026-6-SpiritOfDiscovery-Report.pdf)
- NTSB fishing vessel findings in the search set: Scandies Rose (inaccurate stability instructions), Hotspur (flooding from an unknown source), the 2024 Icy Strait loss (loss of stability, multiple possible factors). Only Progress (2018) has a "considerably larger wave" finding, and that was damage. — [NTSB MAB-19/04](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/MAB1904.pdf); [NTSB MIR-24/03 Hotspur](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/MIR2403.pdf); [Maritime Executive on Scandies Rose](https://maritime-executive.com/article/ntsb-issues-report-on-the-sinking-of-the-scandies-rose)
- Catalogue evidence quality: "The majority of events is based on eyewitness accounts, and as a rule is not confirmed by direct measurements." At the coast, where significant wave height is unknown, events were selected on "unexpectedness and hazard". A reviewer of the 2023 paper cautioned that ERA5 performs poorly in coastal areas with complicated bathymetry. — [ADS abstract, 2011 to 2018 catalogue](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020EGUGA..22.4457D/abstract); [NHESS 2011](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/11/2913/2011/); [NHESS 2023 peer review](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/23/1653/2023/nhess-23-1653-2023-discussion.html)
- A 2022 commentary on Viking Polaris quoted a researcher saying only further investigation would verify whether it met the definition of a rogue wave. — [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2022-12-rogue-cruise-ship-tragedy.html)

### Inferences
- The pattern across NSIA, NTSB, PSA and MAIB is that the remedy investigators recommend is structural (window pressure ratings, air gap, stability) or operational planning for forecast weather. None of the reports found recommends wave-by-wave or rogue-wave forecasting as the missing barrier.
- "Rogue wave" in press reports is usually the operator's or passengers' word. Without a wave record the 2x significant-height criterion cannot be tested, so even careful scientific reconstructions (Louis Majesty) decline to classify.
- Catalogue totals are therefore an upper bound on true rogue-wave harm in one sense (unverified labelling) and a lower bound in another (unreported events, especially in regions with thin English-language media).

### Gaps
- I found no BSU, ATSB or TSB Canada report with an explicit rogue or abnormal wave causal finding. That reflects the search results, not a full review of those archives.
- The Maltese flag-state report on Louis Majesty and any Danish or Norwegian report on MS Maud were not located.

## 3. Statistical estimates: MaxWave "200 ships", accident catalogues, and the sea states in which accidents occur

### Takeaway
The "200+ large ships in two decades" figure is an ESA press-release statement about severe weather, with rogue waves "believed" to be behind many; no underlying loss list was found, and a MaxWave-linked paper gives 22 supercarriers for 1969 to 1994. Accident-database work (Toffoli et al. 2005) shows weather casualties cluster in steep, crossing or rapidly changing seas, often at modest wave heights, which is a sea-state hazard and not proof of individual rogue waves.

### Cited Findings
- ESA, July 2004: "Severe weather has sunk more than 200 supertankers and container ships exceeding 200 metres in length during the last two decades. Rogue waves are believed to be the major cause in many such cases." — [ESA release](https://www.esa.int/Applications/Observing_the_Earth/Ship-sinking_monster_waves_revealed_by_ESA_satellites)
- A later ESA feature softens it to rogue waves "may have caused as many as 200" such ships to sink between 1984 and 2004. — [ESA: Cresting the ocean waves](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Preparing_for_the_Future/Space_for_Earth/Blue_worlds/Cresting_the_ocean_waves)
- Press repetition hardens it further, for example "more than 200 supercarriers have been lost at sea" with rogue waves responsible per eyewitness reports. — [The Register, 2004](https://www.theregister.com/2004/07/23/waves_space/); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna5491071)
- A MaxWave-related academic paper reports nearly 22 supercarriers lost to freak waves from 1969 to 1994, a far smaller count. — [Rosenthal and Lehner, "Rogue Waves: Results of the MaxWave Project"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225019592_Rogue_Waves_Results_of_the_MaxWave_Project)
- MaxWave's measured result: more than ten individual waves above 25 m in three weeks of ERS-2 SAR imagettes. — [CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/22361-esa-satellites-home-in-on-rogue-waves)
- Toffoli, Lefèvre, Bitner-Gregersen and Monbaliu (Applied Ocean Research 27, 2005): accidents taken from Lloyd's Marine Information Service for 1995 to 1999; secondary sources describe 270 weather-related accidents analysed. Sea-state factors identified: significant wave height, wave steepness, crossing seas and rapid development of the sea state. — [VLIZ copy of paper](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/230636.pdf); [NHESS 2019 preprint citing it](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2019-399/nhess-2019-399.pdf)
- Citing papers report that more than 50% of those incidents occurred in sea states with steepness above 0.035, about 60% in the 0.03 to 0.045 band, and that many accidents occurred when significant wave height was below the 3 m commonly taken as the threshold for severe conditions. — [NHESS 2019 preprint](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2019-399/nhess-2019-399.pdf); [Ocean & Coastal Management 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0964569125003850)
- A DNV GL slide deck summarises the same line of work as about 200 ship accidents combined with wind fields and sea conditions, with many in crossing seas and increased rogue-wave probability at crossing angles of 40° to 60°. — [Bitner-Gregersen and Gramstad slides](https://www.wits.ac.za/media/wits-university/conferences/misgsa/documents/2019/2019%204%204%20%20Rogue%20Waves%20FINAL.pdf)
- DNV GL position paper 05-2015: rogue waves "represent operational risks to ship and offshore structures, and are likely to be responsible for a number of accidents." — [cited in Ocean Science 22, 367 (2026)](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/22/367/2026/)
- Class rules do not yet include rogue waves or climate change, owing to no full consensus on rogue wave probability of occurrence. — [Bitner-Gregersen et al., Ocean Engineering](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002980181730745X)
- 2005 to 2021 catalogue location split: about 224 of 429 events (52%) were on the coast; the extract is truncated after "including 82 (19%) on…". — [NHESS 2023 preprint](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2022-215/nhess-2022-215.pdf)
- 2011 to 2018 catalogue: "most often rogue waves caused damages on the shore (on stones or cliffs)"; one summary puts deep-water incidents at about 49% of fatalities. — [EGU2020 presentation](https://presentations.copernicus.org/EGU2020/EGU2020-4457_presentation.pdf); [HSE summary](https://perm.hse.ru/en/news/848838402.html)
- Model limits: ERA5 and the ECMWF CY47R1 hindcast cluster near a maximum-to-significant wave height ratio of about 2, while buoys show more values above 2.5; the authors read this as rogue waves being under-represented in both model datasets. — [Ocean Science 22, 367 (2026)](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/22/367/2026/)

### Inferences
- The 200-ship figure should be cited as "severe weather losses of ships over 200 m, 1984 to 2004, per ESA press material", not as a rogue-wave loss count. The share caused by abnormal waves is unknown.
- I found no published estimate of the fraction of heavy-weather casualties that involve an individual abnormal wave. The Toffoli-type evidence supports "dangerous sea state" warnings (steepness, crossing seas, rapid change), which is a different product from predicting single rogue waves.
- About half of catalogued rogue-wave events and a comparable share of deaths occur at the shoreline, so deep-water ship prediction addresses at most roughly half of the catalogued harm.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Toffoli paper. Sample size is reported inconsistently in secondary sources (650 in the database, 270 analysed, about 200 in DNV slides) and the exact crossing-sea percentage was not retrievable.
- The often-quoted "525 fatalities" for the 22 supercarriers (Kharif and Pelinovsky 2003) was not confirmed in any search result.
- The 2023 NHESS paper's distribution of significant wave height at event times (ERA5) was not retrievable.
- EXTREME SEAS project accident statistics were not found.

## 4. Scale compared with other causes in maritime casualty statistics

### Takeaway
Rogue waves are a small slice of a declining total. Lloyd's List Intelligence data via Allianz show 43 total losses of ships over 100 GT in 2025 and 905 over 2016 to 2025; foundering is the largest cause category (roughly 40 to 50%), and extreme weather is flagged as a factor in at least 7 to 8 losses a year, but "rogue wave" is not a category in any of these datasets. Machinery failure, collision and fire dominate incident counts, and in 2025 to 2026 the industry's headline risk was geopolitical.

### Cited Findings
- Allianz Safety and Shipping Review 2026 (data to year-end 2025, as of 8 June 2026): 43 total losses in 2025 for vessels over 100 GT, more than 30 of them over 500 GT; 905 total losses over 2016 to 2025; 350 in 2021 to 2025 (average 70 a year, 37% down on the previous five years). — [Allianz SSR 2026 PDF](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/commercial-safety-shipping-review-2026.pdf); [Allianz news release](https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/news/safety-shipping-review-2026.html)
- Same edition, all incidents in 2025: machinery damage or failure 1,505 (over half), collision 260, fire/explosion 218; fires were the second-highest annual total of the decade with at least nine total losses. — [Allianz SSR 2026 PDF](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/commercial-safety-shipping-review-2026.pdf)
- A chart label in the 2026 PDF reads "Foundered (sunk) (41%)"; the extract does not show whether this is 2025 or the decade. — [Allianz SSR 2026 PDF](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/commercial-safety-shipping-review-2026.pdf)
- Methodology change: LLI updated its total-loss methodology during 2025, which revised earlier years (one outlet reports 2024 restated to 79 losses; for ships over 500 GT, 51 in 2024 and 31 in 2025). — [Puente de Mando](https://www.puentedemando.com/en/allianz-43-ship-losses-2025/); [Allianz SSR 2026 PDF](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/commercial-safety-shipping-review-2026.pdf)
- Allianz SSR 2025 (data as of 31 March 2025, old methodology): 27 total losses in 2024, 681 over 2015 to 2024, 105 in 2015. Foundered was the main cause in 2024 (12 losses, close to 50%); over the decade foundering accounted for 50% and fire/explosion 26%. Fishing vessels were close to 40% of 2024 losses (10), cargo 6. — [Allianz SSR 2025 PDF](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/commercial-safety-shipping-review-2025.pdf); [Splash247](https://splash247.com/maritime-losses-reach-historic-low-amid-rising-geopolitical-and-technological-risks-allianz/)
- Weather as a reported factor in total losses (Allianz, minimum counts): at least 7 in 2024; at least 8 in 2023; at least 8 in 2022; at least 25% of losses in 2021; about one in five in 2019. — [SSR 2025](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/commercial-safety-shipping-review-2025.pdf); [SSR 2024](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/Commercial-Safety-Shipping-Review-2024.pdf); [SSR 2023](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/AGCS-Safety-Shipping-Review-2023.pdf); [Allianz claims trends](https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/expert-risk-articles/marine-claims-trends.html); [SSR 2020](https://globalmaritimehub.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/AGCS-Safety-Shipping-Review-2020.pdf)
- IUMI (older data): weather was the major cause of total hull losses, "almost 50% of the vessels lost between 2009 and 2013", with grounding second at 25%. — [SAFETY4SEA on IUMI statistics](https://safety4sea.com/iumi-statistics-show-total-hull-loss-fall/)
- IUMI 2025 report: 2024 headline was fires; claims occurring during ocean voyages rose from a long-term average of 25% to 37% in 2024. — [IUMI Stats Report 2025](https://iumi.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/IUMI-Stats-Report-2025.pdf)
- EMSA Annual Overview 2026 (EU-flagged ships and EU waters, 2016 to 2025): 2,630 occurrences in 2025; 52 very serious casualties; 29 deaths, the lowest of the decade (95 in 2016; 2021 to 2025 average 47); 813 injuries; 16 ships lost. — [SAFETY4SEA](https://safety4sea.com/emsa-annual-overview-of-marine-casualties-and-incidents-2026/); [MaritimeCyprus](https://maritimecyprus.com/2026/10/14/emsa-annual-overview-of-marine-casualties-and-incidents-2026/)
- EMSA 2026, per a secondary summary I could not verify: human action about 64% of classified accident events in finalised investigations, system or equipment failure about 23%. — [IIMS summary](https://www.iims.org.uk/emsa-releases-annual-overview-of-marine-casualties-and-incidents-2026/)
- The 2026 Allianz review's emphasis is geopolitical (Strait of Hormuz closure, $125 bn of vessel and cargo value awaiting passage from the Persian Gulf). — [Allianz news release](https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/news/safety-shipping-review-2026.html)

### Inferences
- Order of magnitude: catalogued rogue-wave sinkings run at about 3 vessels a year including small boats under 100 GT, against 43 to about 90 total losses a year of ships over 100 GT from all causes. Even if every catalogued sinking were a ship over 100 GT, rogue waves would be a single-digit percentage of total losses. This is my comparison of two differently defined datasets, so treat it as indicative only.
- "Foundered" and "weather was a factor" are both far broader than "rogue wave". Foundering includes flooding, cargo shift and liquefaction, structural failure and stability loss in ordinary heavy weather.
- Total losses have fallen by well over half in a decade without any rogue-wave forecasting, which suggests design, regulation and routing improvements have driven the gains.

### Gaps
- I could not extract the 2016 to 2025 total-loss cause table from the 2026 Allianz PDF, so the decade foundering count under the new methodology is unconfirmed.
- EMSA's weather or environmental contributing-factor share was not retrievable; the report has a section on it.
- No Lloyd's List Intelligence figure isolating weather as a share of casualties for 2020 to 2025 was found, and no Cefor heavy-weather claims cost figures.
- No source puts a monetary value on rogue-wave losses specifically.

## 5. Container loss at sea and parametric rolling: linked to rogue waves?

### Takeaway
No. The investigated large container-loss events are attributed to parametric rolling and related stability and stowage failures, which arise from regular wave trains interacting with ship speed, heading and roll period, sometimes in mild seas. Annual totals are small relative to traffic and are driven by one or two casualties a year.

### Cited Findings
- World Shipping Council 2026 update (published 25 June 2026): 1,478 containers lost in 2025, up from 576 in 2024; about 280 million containers carried in 2025, a loss rate of about 0.0005%. — [WSC release](https://www.worldshipping.org/news/world-shipping-council-releases-containers-lost-at-sea-report-2026-update); [Supply Chain 24/7](https://www.supplychain247.com/article/world-shipping-council-containers-lost-at-sea-2026-report)
- One casualty, MSC Elsa 3 (capsized and sank about 38 nm off India, late May 2025), accounts for 640 of the 2025 total, about 43%. — [SWZ Maritime](https://swzmaritime.nl/news/2026/07/01/1478-containers-lost-at-sea-in-2025-above-three-year-average/); [trans.info](https://trans.info/en/container-losses-2025-485999)
- Historical high was 5,578 containers in 2013. 128 containers were recovered in 2025. WSC says annual totals swing on a small number of large incidents; the 2025 report cites North Atlantic and North Pacific weather and fire-related incidents. — [Hellenic Shipping News](https://www.hellenicshippingnews.com/world-shipping-council-releases-containers-lost-at-sea-report-2026-update/)
- From 1 January 2026, SOLAS amendments require reporting of containers lost or seen drifting, and flag states must report to the IMO. — [WSC release](https://www.worldshipping.org/news/world-shipping-council-releases-containers-lost-at-sea-report-2026-update)
- TopTier joint industry project (MARIN), June 2025 summary: analysis of 44 incidents over 20 years involving nearly 10,000 containers found parametric rolling the most likely cause of large incidents; single-bay losses were often due to local failures and stack resonance. — [TT Club](https://www.ttclub.com/news-and-resources/news/article/tt-talk-industry-reports-shed-new-light-on-container-losses-at-sea/)
- TopTier notice to mariners: parametric rolling in following seas is especially hazardous; "unfavourable combinations of rolling period, vessel speed, heading, and wave conditions, can trigger sudden and extremely rapid increase of roll motions"; it can occur in relatively mild wave heights. — [TopTier notice (MARIN)](https://www.marin.nl/api/marin/downloads/get-download/6475/0/2430/block_files_vr2puklqj6p91/NpTF0n8Wzwohy5FuVpWdBLJku6k=/TopTier_N2M_ParamatricRollFollowingSeas_V1.2.pdf); [SAFETY4SEA](https://safety4sea.com/parametric-rolling-in-following-seas-what-to-be-aware-of/)
- TopTier also estimates about half of annual container losses come from frequent small incidents involving overweight, poorly packed or damaged containers or worn lashing gear. — [The Loadstar](https://theloadstar.com/container-safety-in-motion-as-industry-moves-to-cut-losses/)
- Maersk Essen (January 2021): DMAIB concluded the ship most likely experienced parametric resonance in port quartering seas, possibly combined with pure loss of stability on a wave crest. — [TSB Canada report M21P0297 (ZIM Kingston), which reviews comparable cases](https://www.tsb.gc.ca/eng/rapports-reports/marine/2021/m21p0297/m21p0297.html)
- The same TSB report quotes other investigations: one loss "was almost certainly the consequence of parametric rolling", and an MAIB conclusion that accelerations from excessive heavy rolling combined with weight-distribution discrepancies caused progressive stack collapse. The extracts do not name the ships. — [TSB Canada M21P0297](https://www.tsb.gc.ca/eng/rapports-reports/marine/2021/m21p0297/m21p0297.html)
- ONE Apus (30 November 2020, North Pacific): secondary sources describe rolls of 25 to 27 degrees in 5 to 6 m swell triggering parametric rolling and stack collapse beyond lashing limits. — [Wikipedia: ONE Apus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ONE_Apus); [FreightAmigo](https://www.freightamigo.com/en/blog/logistics/the-one-apus-accident-carrier-and-consignees-awaiting-final-resolution/)

### Inferences
- Parametric rolling depends on swell period, encounter frequency and ship loading, all of which are forecastable from standard spectral wave forecasts plus ship data. The relevant mitigation is roll-risk guidance and lashing integrity, not rogue-wave prediction.
- Because one casualty can be 40% or more of a year's losses, year-to-year container-loss totals say little about wave climate.

### Gaps
- ATSB (APL England, 2020) and MAIB (CMA CGM G. Washington, 2018) findings were not retrieved directly, so I do not state them.
- The investigating body for ONE Apus is given inconsistently in secondary sources (JTSB versus MAIB).
- The cause of the MSC Elsa 3 capsize was not researched here.
- WSC multi-year averages (3-year, 10-year) for the 2026 update were not retrievable.

## 6. Small craft, fishing vessels and coastal or shore incidents relative to deep-water ship incidents

### Takeaway
The shoreline and small-boat categories are at least as large as the deep-water ship category in the rogue-wave catalogues, and local data sets for single coasts (Australian rock fishing, US Pacific Northwest sneaker waves) show steady annual deaths comparable to the global ship-related totals. All of it is tiny beside total fishing-sector mortality.

### Cited Findings
- 2005 to 2021 catalogue: about 52% of 429 events were on the coast; ships sunk included "both small fishing boats and large ships". — [NHESS 2023 preprint](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2022-215/nhess-2022-215.pdf); [NHESS 2023](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/23/1653/2023/)
- 2011 to 2018 catalogue: damage occurred most often on the shore; hotspots were the coasts of the USA, the Caribbean, Europe, Australia and New Zealand. — [EGU2020 presentation](https://presentations.copernicus.org/EGU2020/EGU2020-4457_presentation.pdf); [HSE summary](https://perm.hse.ru/en/news/848838402.html)
- Sneaker waves, Oregon and northern California, 2005 to 2017: 29 fatalities (15 Oregon, 14 northern California). An earlier abstract gives 27 since 2005. Oregon-only counts: 21 killed since 1990, or 20 between 2000 and 2018. — [García-Medina et al. catalogue](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326217800_Analysis_and_catalogue_of_sneaker_waves_in_the_US_Pacific_Northwest_between_2005_and_2017); [AGU abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018AGUOSCD14B0033G/abstract); [EARTH Magazine](https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/taking-surprise-out-sneaker-waves/)
- Australian rock fishing: 185 drowning deaths and 7 other fatalities (192 total) recorded nationally since 2004 in the 2020 brief, averaging 13 a year; a later SLSA item says 12 a year. It is consistently the third-highest activity for coastal drowning. — [SLSA Coastal Safety Brief: Rock Fishing (2020)](https://www.surflifesaving.com.au/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2020/05/Surf-Life-Saving-Australia-Rock-Fishing-Coastal-Safety-Brief-2020.pdf); [SLSA](https://sls.com.au/rock-fishers-at-risk-in-australias-most-dangerous-sport/)
- Australia, National Drowning Report 2026: 313 drowning deaths in Australian waterways; rock locations were 6% of all drowning deaths, down 42% on the previous year and 22% below the 10-year average. — [Royal Life Saving National Drowning Report 2026](https://www.royallifesaving.com.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/133268/National-Drowning-Report-2026.pdf)
- Global fishing mortality: more than 100,000 fishing-related deaths a year (FISH Safety Foundation for Pew, November 2022), against the older ILO estimate of 24,000 and other estimates of 24,000 to 32,000. — [Pew](https://www.pew.org/en/about/news-room/press-releases-and-statements/2022/11/03/more-than-100000-people-die-annually-across-global-fishing-sector-new-research-shows); [Willis et al. 2023, Marine Policy](https://www.ldac.eu/images/Willis_et_al_2023_The_human_cost_of_global_fishing.pdf)
- Fishing vessels were close to 40% of total losses over 100 GT in 2024 (10 of 27, old methodology). — [Allianz SSR 2025](https://commercial.allianz.com/content/dam/onemarketing/commercial/commercial/reports/commercial-safety-shipping-review-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- Two regional shore datasets alone (about 13 a year for Australian rock fishing, about 2 a year for Oregon and northern California sneaker waves) add up to a figure of the same order as a third of the global catalogued rogue-wave death rate of about 39 a year. Not all rock-fishing deaths involve an unexpected wave, so this is an order-of-magnitude comparison only.
- Catalogued rogue-wave deaths (about 40 a year) are under 0.2% of even the lowest global fishing death estimate (24,000 a year). Fishing deaths are dominated by other factors, so better rogue-wave prediction would not move that total noticeably.
- Shore victims have no ship-borne sensors or routing; mitigation there is beach hazard forecasting, signage and lifejackets, which is a different product from open-ocean rogue-wave prediction.

### Gaps
- No global count of sneaker-wave or shore-wave deaths exists in the sources found; only regional series.
- The share of rock-fishing deaths caused specifically by an unexpectedly large wave was not found.
- Small-craft and recreational boat casualties attributed to rogue waves are not separately tabulated in any source retrieved.

## 7. Is rogue wave frequency or risk changing with climate change or shipping routes?

### Takeaway
There is evidence that extreme wave heights are rising in the Southern Ocean and will rise sharply in an ice-free Arctic, but no robust evidence that rogue waves (relative to the sea state) are becoming more frequent; the one long buoy study found occurrence mostly decreasing with severity increasing, and the IPCC rates North Atlantic extreme-wave projections as low confidence. Exposure is changing mainly because expedition cruising and Arctic transits put more ships in rough regions.

### Cited Findings
- Southern Ocean, observed: over about twenty years wind speeds rose around 10% and extreme wave heights around 6 to 7%. — [ESA Climate Office](https://climate.esa.int/en/news-events/ocean-wave-patterns-present-evolving-threat/)
- Southern Ocean, projected: the 1-in-100-year significant wave height increases by the end of the century under both scenarios studied, by roughly 15% under RCP 8.5. — [Eos](https://eos.org/articles/larger-waves-in-store-as-the-planet-warms)
- Arctic, projected: extreme wave events that occurred once every 20 years may occur once every 2 to 5 years on average, driven by sea-ice loss exposing more open water to wind. — [AGU press release on Casas-Prat and Wang 2020](https://news.agu.org/press-release/climate-change-may-cause-extreme-waves-in-arctic/); [JGR Oceans](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2019JC015745)
- Rogue waves specifically, US west coast buoys: "rogue wave occurrence displays a mostly decreasing trend, but the relative height – or severity – of the waves is increasing." — [Cattrell et al. 2019, Scientific Reports](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6418161/)
- Same study, baseline exposure extrapolated to main North Atlantic shipping routes: 0.8 to 1.2% per day likelihood of encountering a rogue wave exceeding 11 m. — [Cattrell et al. 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6418161/)
- North Atlantic projections: a 2017 study using several climate models found some locations could see more rogue-prone sea states; it covered selected locations only. — ["Projected Changes in the Occurrence of Extreme and Rogue Waves in Future Climate in the North Atlantic" (ResearchGate record; authorship not confirmed in this session)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320200436_Projected_Changes_in_the_Occurrence_of_Extreme_and_Rogue_Waves_in_Future_Climate_in_the_North_Atlantic)
- IPCC as reported: low confidence in projected changes in North Atlantic extreme wave conditions "due to limited evidence"; significant wave heights projected to increase in the Southern Ocean and tropical eastern Pacific and decrease over much of the Atlantic and Mediterranean; one expert view is that the locations where rogue waves occur more often may shift with storm tracks. — [Phys.org, 2022](https://phys.org/news/2022-12-rogue-cruise-ship-tragedy.html)
- ExWaMar project (results published 2023 to 2024): 30-year past and future simulations under RCP 4.5 and 8.5, aimed at improved warning criteria for increased risk of extreme and rogue waves. — [Ocean Engineering](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002980182302927X)
- Regional exposure: NSIA noted wind speeds capable of producing breaking waves are common in the Drake Passage for much of the year, and trade coverage says abnormally large waves are a regular occurrence there. — [Marine Log](https://www.marinelog.com/legal/safety-and-security/video-nsia-reports-on-deadly-viking-polaris-encounter-with-a-breaking-wave/); [Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/report-windows-aboard-expedition-cruise-ship-couldn-t-resist-fatal-wave)
- Britannia P&I published a 2025 white paper on climate change, severe weather and shipping risk. — [Britannia P&I](https://britanniapandi.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Climate-change-Severe-weather-and-its-impact-on-shipping-risks.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rising significant wave height raises absolute wave heights without changing the rogue ratio, so the practical risk signal is in the background sea state (which is forecastable), not in a changing rogue-wave rate.
- The recent fatal and damaging cruise cases (Viking Polaris 2022 in the Drake Passage, MS Maud 2023 in a North Sea storm) fit a pattern of more passenger ships operating in high-latitude rough water. Growth of expedition cruising is a plausible driver of exposure, but I found no dataset quantifying it against incident counts.

### Gaps
- No 2023 to 2026 peer-reviewed study quantifying a trend in rogue-wave occurrence along shipping routes, in the Arctic or in the Southern Ocean was found.
- ExWaMar's quantitative future-occurrence results were not retrieved.
- No source attributes any specific casualty or loss trend to climate-driven changes in rogue waves.
- Arctic and Southern Ocean traffic growth figures were outside what the searches returned.
